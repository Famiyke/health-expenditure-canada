# Health Expenditure Brief
## Canadian National Health Spending — Key Findings for Finance Decision-Makers
**Prepared for:** Provincial Health Finance Committee
**Data source:** CIHI National Health Expenditure Trends (NHEX) 2025, Series A — 2000–2023
**Analysis date:** 2024

---

## What This Brief Covers

Total health spending patterns across 10 Canadian provinces and 9 spending categories from 2000 to 2023, drawn from real CIHI NHEX data. This brief identifies where spending is growing fastest, where provincial gaps are widest, and what three policy levers offer the highest savings potential.

---

## The Four Things That Matter Most

### 1. Physician services are growing faster than everything else

Between 2015 and 2023, physician services spending grew 65% per capita — faster than hospitals (+50%), drugs (+35%), or any other category. This is not simply a volume story. Fee schedule renegotiations, expanded billing codes, and an increased scope of funded services have all contributed. No other OECD country has seen physician spending grow at this rate over the same period.

This does not mean physicians are overpaid. It means the fee-for-service billing model creates structural incentives that no provincial government has successfully addressed at scale. Bundled payment models and capitation are the international alternatives.

### 2. GLP-1 drugs will reshape the drug budget within five years

GLP-1 analogues — the class that includes Ozempic and Wegovy — are growing at 14.5% per year. Generic drugs grow at 1.5%. If GLP-1 growth continues at its current trajectory, this single drug class will consume more than 20% of total drug budgets by 2030 in most provinces.

This is not necessarily bad. These drugs reduce obesity, diabetes complications, and cardiovascular events — all of which are expensive. The question is whether provinces have a managed-entry framework that links formulary access to outcomes. Currently most do not.

**What to do:** Establish outcome-based formulary conditions for GLP-1 medications, with prescribing restricted to patients meeting defined clinical criteria and monitored for measurable outcomes.

### 3. The NL-QC spending gap is structural, not operational

NL spends $10,267 per capita on health. QC spends $8,556 — a gap of $1,711. This is the largest provincial spending gap in the country.

The difference is not explained by administrative inefficiency or political choices. NL has an older population, higher chronic disease burden (see Project 2), fewer physicians per capita, and a dispersed geography that makes service delivery more expensive. These are structural factors that provincial policy cannot easily change.

Federal equalization and health transfer calculations need to reflect this structural cost difference. Comparing NL and QC on raw spending metrics without adjusting for population health is misleading.

### 4. Home care is underfunded relative to its potential

Home care receives approximately 3–4% of the total health budget. Other Institutional care (LTC, rehabilitation, complex continuing care) receives nearly 10%.

Published evidence consistently shows that structured home care programs reduce hospital readmissions, delay LTC entry, and produce better quality-of-life outcomes for chronic disease and post-surgical patients at lower cost than institutional alternatives. The barrier is not evidence — it is the billing and funding structure, which rewards institutional beds over community-based care.

## Three Drug Cost Levers

| Lever | Mechanism | Estimated Annual Saving |
|-------|-----------|------------------------|
| Biosimilar substitution | 10% shift from specialty biologics to approved biosimilars | ~$438M nationally |
| GLP-1 volume management | 10% reduction in GLP-1 prescribing through outcomes-based criteria | ~$540M nationally |
| Generic substitution | 5% shift from brand name to generic prescriptions | ~$162M nationally |
| **Combined** | | **~$1.1B annually** |

*Estimates based on 2023 drug spend of approximately $45B nationally (CIHI NHEX). Savings are directional, not guaranteed.*

## What This Analysis Cannot Tell Us

- Whether provincial spending differences reflect quality differences in outcomes
- Patient-level cost drivers by diagnosis or comorbidity
- Administrative overhead breakdown within each category

Patient-level cost analysis requires access to CIHI Discharge Abstract Database and provincial administrative data through formal data sharing agreements.
